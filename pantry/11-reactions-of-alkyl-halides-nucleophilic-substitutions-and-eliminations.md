## Hook scenes

- **Walden's malic acid cycle (1896).** Pure (−)-malic acid → PCl₅ → (+)-chlorosuccinic acid → wet Ag₂O → (+)-malic acid. The configuration flipped, but Walden couldn't see *which step* did it. Forty years until Hughes & Ingold solved it. Emil Fischer's "most remarkable observation" quote. Reusable as a hook for any chapter on stereochemistry, on the slow accretion of mechanistic understanding, or on what optical rotation can and can't tell you.

- **The (CH₃)₃CBr / CH₃Br rate paradox.** Tertiary halide with water beats methyl halide with hydroxide by a factor of one million. Reusable as a reactivity-puzzle hook anywhere a "more hindered substrate is faster" story is the right kind of strangeness.

- **Menthyl vs. neomenthyl chloride.** Same atoms, different stereochemistry, 200-fold rate difference and a complete switch in product. Reusable as a hook for *conformation-controlled chemistry* or anywhere you want to make the case that geometry can dominate over thermodynamic preference.

## Specification moves

- **"SN1 / SN2 / E1 / E2" — what each subscript and number means.** The S vs. E names which kind of reaction (substitution vs. elimination); the N specifies that the substitution is *nucleophilic* (not radical or electrophilic); the 1 vs. 2 names the *molecularity of the rate-determining step*, not the number of steps in the whole mechanism. SN1 has two steps; SN2 has one; both use the suffix "1" or "2" to mean unimolecular or bimolecular *rate-determining*. The numbers are kinetic, not structural.

- **"Nucleophile vs. base."** The same species (HO⁻, RO⁻, ⁻NH₂) can attack a carbon (acting as nucleophile, leading to substitution) or attack a hydrogen (acting as base, leading to elimination). Nothing distinguishes the two roles structurally — they're the same lone pair attacking different positions. The substrate's geometry, the steric environment, and the temperature decide which target wins.

- **"Anti-periplanar."** Periplanar = four atoms in the same plane. Anti = on opposite sides. Anti-periplanar = the four atoms (H, C, C, X) lie in the same plane *and* the H/X are on opposite faces. The orbital alignment requirement, not just a geometric coincidence.

- **"Bimolecular" vs. "unimolecular" doesn't mean what the words sound like.** *Unimolecular* in chemistry doesn't mean "involves one molecule total" — it means "one molecule appears in the rate-determining step." SN1 still requires a nucleophile, but the nucleophile shows up after the slow step.

## Analogies

- **Umbrella inversion (SN2).** The transition state has three substituents flat, the new bond forming on one side, the old bond breaking on the other. Snap-through, like a wind-flipped umbrella. Earns its place because it captures both the inversion *and* the planar transition state.

- **Carbocation as flat platform (SN1).** sp² hybridized, three bonds in a plane, empty p-orbital sticking up and down. Achiral by symmetry. Nucleophile lands on either face with equal probability — hence racemization. Earns its place because the geometry directly explains the stereochemical result.

- **Ion pair as a half-detached partner (SN1).** The leaving group hasn't fully diffused away yet. It still partially blocks one face. The nucleophile attacks from the other side preferentially — net inversion. Limit of full separation: random attack, racemization. Limit of no separation: backside attack, full inversion. Real reactions sit somewhere in between, leaning toward inversion.

- **The lever model for solvent effects.** Protic solvents lower the *ground state* of an anion (bad for SN2, neutral for SN1). Polar protic solvents lower the *transition state* for ion formation (good for SN1, neutral for SN2). The same solvent change moves opposite levers — that's why solvent is the most powerful single variable for switching mechanisms.

## Numbers worth keeping

- (CH₃)₃CBr + H₂O is **>10⁶ times faster** than CH₃Br + HO⁻. The puzzle that demanded SN1.
- **5 orders of magnitude** between water and cyanide as nucleophiles toward CH₃Br (relative rates 1 vs. 125,000).
- **200,000×** rate increase for azide + 1-bromobutane on going from methanol to HMPA.
- **100,000×** rate increase for 2-chloro-2-methylpropane on going from ethanol to water.
- **kH/kD = 7.11** for E2 of 1-bromo-2-phenylethane vs. its dideuterio analog.
- **200×** rate difference between neomenthyl chloride and menthyl chloride E2.
- **64:36 SN1:E1** for 2-chloro-2-methylpropane in 80% aqueous ethanol at 65°C.
- **80% racemization, 20% inversion** for SN1 of (R)-6-chloro-2,6-dimethyloctane in water.
- **~50 kJ/mol** weaker C–X bond at allylic and benzylic positions.

## Diagnostic levers (mechanism-flipping conditions)

These are the single-variable moves that change which mechanism wins. Useful for designing reactions and for diagnosing why a published reaction went the way it did.

- **Bulky base (e.g., *t*-BuO⁻, LDA)** → forces E2 even on primary substrates.
- **Polar aprotic solvent (DMF, DMSO, MeCN, HMPA)** → boosts SN2 by 4–5 orders of magnitude.
- **Polar protic solvent without strong base (water, alcohols)** → enables SN1/E1 on tertiary, allyl, benzyl.
- **Heating** → favors elimination (entropy: 3 particles vs. 2).
- **β-H acidified by adjacent carbonyl** → enables E1cB.
- **Convert −OH → −OTs (or −Cl, −Br)** → unlocks SN2 chemistry on alcohols.

## Forbidden moves to watch for

- "SN2 reactions are second-order in nucleophile." (No — first-order in each, second-order overall.)
- "Tertiary substrates do SN2 slowly." (No — they don't do SN2 at all.)
- "E1 reactions don't happen if there's no base." (Yes they do — the solvent acts as a weak base in the second step. SN1 and E1 always co-occur in protic solvents.)
- "Backside attack means from behind." (No — opposite to the leaving group.)
- "The carbocation is the transition state." (No — the carbocation is an intermediate sitting in an energy valley; the transition states sit on either side of it.)
